A Tokyo Restaurant Guide for You

A hand-picked list of favorite places to eat — from beloved everyday chains to century-old institutions — compiled with love (and strong opinions) by your friend.

❖ ❖ ❖

🇬🇪 A Taste of Home, the Japanese Way — შქმერული at Matsuya

Yes, really: to celebrate its 60th anniversary, the beef-bowl chain Matsuya brings back its famous shkmeruli set meal (シュクメルリ鍋定食) — a limited-quantity run starting June 16, 2026, sold at Matsuya stores worldwide (from ¥990; the full hot-pot set meal is ¥1,190). It won first place in Matsuya's "revival menu election" and Japan adores it. Fair warning from your friend: it is absolutely not traditional shkmeruli — go, taste it, and be lovingly outraged. 😂

How to Use This Guide

使い方
  • Chains are everywhere. For any restaurant in the first section, just open Google Maps and search the name — there will almost certainly be one near you.
  • Carry some cash. Most places take cards or IC cards now, but small older restaurants (especially in Omoide Yokocho or Tsukiji) can be cash-only.
  • Queues are normal. Japanese people queue for good food as a hobby. Go before 11:30 for popular lunch spots, and avoid weekends where noted.
  • Lunch is the bargain. Many high-end restaurants serve a lunch version of their dinner menu for a third of the price — even a Michelin-starred one below serves an ~¥800–1,000 lunch.
  • Ticket machines. Many ramen and casual shops have a vending machine at the entrance: buy a ticket first, then hand it to the staff.
  • “〜本店 (honten)” means main/original branch — usually the one worth seeking out.

Big Chain Restaurants

大手チェーン

Don't dismiss chains in Japan — they are cheap, fast, open late (some 24h), and far better than the word “chain” suggests. Since most of these don't exist in Georgia, they're experiences in themselves. All of them have stores everywhere: just search the name on Google Maps.

Matsuya 松屋

Gyūdon · Set Meals¥500–1,200

One of Japan's “big three” beef-bowl (gyūdon) chains. Order from a ticket machine, get free miso soup with set meals, and eat well for under ¥1,000. Open very late or 24 hours in many locations.

Friend's note: They're selling შქმერული for a limited time from mid-June (see the banner above — it actually starts June 16!). As a Georgian you will probably be angry, because it's definitely not the traditional შქმერული 😂

Sukiya すき家

Gyūdon¥400–1,000

The largest gyūdon chain in Japan by store count. Famous for its endless topping variations — cheese, kimchi, green onion & egg — on a bowl of simmered beef over rice.

Yoshinoya 吉野家

Gyūdon¥400–1,000

The original — founded in 1899 at the old Nihonbashi fish market. The classic, no-frills beef bowl that defined the genre. Orange signs, counter seats, food in about a minute.

Yayoi-ken やよい軒

Teishoku Set Meals¥700–1,300

Proper home-style Japanese teishoku (set meals): a main dish, miso soup, pickles, and — the killer feature — free unlimited rice refills from a self-serve machine. The closest thing to Japanese home cooking in chain form.

Tendon Tenya 天丼てんや

Tempura · Tendon¥600–1,200

Crispy tempura over rice (tendon) at fast-food prices. A bowl with shrimp, squid, and seasonal vegetables drizzled in sweet-savory sauce costs around ¥700 — astonishing value for tempura, which is normally an expensive dish.

Marugame Seimen 丸亀製麺

Sanuki Udon¥400–900

Self-service udon chain where thick, chewy Sanuki-style noodles are kneaded and boiled in-house, in front of you. Slide your tray along, pick tempura and onigiri from the counter, and pay at the end. Fast, cheap, and genuinely good.

CoCo Ichibanya CoCo壱番屋

Japanese Curry¥700–1,500

The world's largest curry restaurant chain — and the standard-bearer of Japanese curry rice. Choose your rice amount, spice level (1–10), and pile on toppings like pork cutlet, cheese, or fried chicken. Endlessly customizable comfort food.

Friend's note: THE place to understand what “Japanese curry” means to ordinary people.

Sushiro / Kura Sushi / Hama Sushi スシロー・くら寿司・はま寿司

Conveyor-belt Sushi~¥120–¥1,500 total

The big three kaiten-zushi (conveyor-belt sushi) chains. Plates start around ¥120–150, you order extras from a touch screen (with English), and the plates arrive on a little express lane. A fun, cheap, zero-intimidation way to eat a lot of sushi.

Friend's note: If it's your first time at a conveyor-belt sushi bar, go to Kura Sushi. Every five empty plates you feed into the slot gives you one chance at a capsule-toy game. It's pretty hard to actually win one, but the animation is funny.

Ringer Hut リンガーハット

Nagasaki Chanpon¥700–1,200

Specialist in chanpon — a Nagasaki-born noodle soup in its own genre, distinct from ramen: thick noodles in a milky pork-and-seafood broth, topped with a mountain of stir-fried cabbage, vegetables, and seafood. Their veggie-loaded version uses 100% domestic vegetables.

Friend's note: Probably not “Japanese ramen” as most people picture it, but chanpon is its own thing — definitely worth trying.

Ichiran 一蘭

Tonkotsu Ramen¥1,000–1,800

Born as a Fukuoka ramen stall in 1960, Ichiran is the world-famous temple of tonkotsu (pork-bone) ramen — and of solo dining. You order from a ticket machine, fine-tune your bowl on a paper form (broth intensity, richness, garlic, spice, noodle firmness), then eat in a private “flavor-concentration booth” where the bowl appears from behind a curtain. Many locations are open very late or 24 hours.

Friend's note: I honestly think Ichiran is a little bit overrated — but lots of people love them, and it's almost a must-try on a first trip to Japan.

Gyukaku 牛角

Yakiniku · Grill-it-yourself¥3,000–6,000

Japan's best-known yakiniku chain: you grill marinated beef, pork, and vegetables yourself over a charcoal or gas grill at your table. Great fun in a group, with all-you-can-eat courses available. The Japanese take on Korean BBQ.

Torikizoku 鳥貴族

Yakitori · Izakaya~¥390/item

The largest chain izakaya (Japanese pub) in the country, devoted to yakitori — charcoal-grilled chicken skewers. Everything on the menu, food and drinks alike, is one flat price (around ¥390). Loud, cheerful, and the easiest introduction to izakaya culture.

Friend's note: The largest chain izakaya — go hungry and order recklessly; the flat price protects you.

Non-chain & Smaller Restaurants

名店・個店

These are either independent restaurants or much smaller operations. I've grouped them by neighborhood so you can plan your days — Ginza/Tsukiji alone could fill a week. Each name links to a Google Maps search.

Ginza 銀座 — old money, great food

Tonkatsu Ginza Bairin とんかつ 銀座 梅林

Tonkatsu · Katsudon¥1,500–3,000

Ginza's first tonkatsu (breaded pork cutlet) specialist, founded in 1927. Its founder, a former pharmacist, invented the house katsu sauce, and the shop is credited with inventing the katsu sandwich. The katsudon — cutlet and softly set egg over rice — has been named one of the best rice bowls in Japan by Japanese food TV authorities.

📍 Find on Google Maps

Yakitori Kufuraku Ginza Sohonten 焼鳥 くふ楽 銀座総本店

Yakitori · Izakaya¥4,000–7,000

A refined yakitori izakaya one minute from Ginza-Itchome Station, where certified grill masters cook skewers of Michinoku Seiryū chicken from Iwate Prefecture — delivered fresh each morning and grilled over white binchō charcoal: crisp and fragrant outside, juicy inside. Courses from about ¥3,850.

📍 Find on Google Maps

Ginza Hachigō 銀座 八五

Ramen · Michelin Bib Gourmand¥1,200–2,000

A tiny six-seat counter where a chef with 36 years of French fine-dining experience makes ramen like consommé: the broth is clarified and seasoned with cured ham instead of conventional tare, and the wontons are filled with foie gras and truffle. A full-course meal in a bowl, recognized by the Michelin Guide. Expect a queue (English menus handed out while you wait).

📍 Find on Google Maps

Shabu-shabu Yamawarau Ginza しゃぶしゃぶ山笑ふ 銀座店

Shabu-shabuLunch ~¥5,000 · Dinner ~¥7,500

A stylish “solo shabu-shabu” specialist: every diner gets a personal pot at the counter, so you can enjoy this very Japanese hot-pot ritual even alone. Yamagata beef and Yonezawa-pork are swished briefly in broth and dipped in sauces, with certified Niigata rice on the side.

📍 Find on Google Maps

Kissa You 喫茶 you

Kissaten · Omurice¥1,200–2,000

A beloved Shōwa-retro coffeehouse from 1970, right next to the Kabukiza Theatre and a longtime favorite of kabuki actors. Its legendary omurice — an almost liquid, custardy omelette draped over ketchup rice, made rich with two kinds of cream — has been called “drinkable omurice” on Japanese TV. The queue is part of the legend.

Friend's note: The omurice is extremely popular, but you might wait over an hour at least. If you want to go, definitely avoid weekends.

📍 Find on Google Maps

Ginza Kome Ryōtei Hachidaime Gihey 銀座米料亭 八代目儀兵衛

Rice KaisekiLunch ¥2,000–4,000 · Dinner ~¥13,000

A “rice restaurant” run by an eighth-generation Kyoto rice merchant — here, perfectly cooked clay-pot rice is the star and everything else is the supporting cast. Their original blend is cooked in custom clay pots with obsessive attention to water and timing; the moment the lid comes off is theatre. Proof that in Japan, plain white rice can be a destination dish.

📍 Find on Google Maps

Ginza Kagari Honten 銀座 篝 本店

Ramen · Tori Paitan¥1,300–2,000

Hidden in a Ginza back alley, an eight-seat counter that feels more like a chef's bar than a ramen shop. Famous since 2013 for tori paitan soba — a thick, creamy, almost potage-like chicken broth made from 100% domestic chicken, topped with seasonal vegetables. One of Tokyo's iconic ramen bowls; weekday evenings have shorter lines.

📍 Find on Google Maps

Ginza Yoshizawa 銀座 吉澤

Sukiyaki · Matsusaka Beef¥8,000–20,000

Founded in 1926, a restaurant grown out of a butcher's shop specializing in legendary Matsusaka beef — one of Japan's top wagyu brands, rivaling Kobe. Sukiyaki and steak courses showcase the meat, and the attached butcher counter sells beef bentō and souvenirs. Note: lunch teishoku here is a relatively affordable way into Matsusaka beef.

📍 Find on Google Maps

Tonkatsu Aoki, Ginza 8-chōme とんかつ檍 銀座8丁目店

Tonkatsu¥2,000–3,000

A cult favorite among tonkatsu lovers and a Tabelog “Top 100” tonkatsu shop. Aoki uses Hayashi SPF pork, prized for its sweet fat and juiciness, fried to a blushing-pink perfection, and a row of specialty salts on the counter for dipping instead of sauce. Closed Wednesdays.

📍 Find on Google Maps

Tsukiji 築地 — the old market neighborhood

Tsukiji Hachinoko 築地 蜂の子

Yōshoku (Western-Japanese)Lunch ~¥1,500 · Dinner courses

A yōshoku (Japanese-Western) restaurant founded in 1948, tucked in a quiet alley near Tsukiji. By day it serves classic lunches — omurice, crispy fried oysters in fresh breadcrumbs — and by night, Western/French-leaning courses. Its signature “lebagura” is a gratin of chicken-liver stew baked with spaghetti, an only-in-Japan invention.

📍 Find on Google Maps

Tsukiji Fujimura 築地 ふじむら

Seafood · KaisendonLunch ~¥1,300–2,000

A small kappō-style eatery serving fish brought in daily from the market: brilliant-value sashimi lunches, seafood rice bowls (kaisendon), and — in season — enormous fried oysters limited to about 20 servings a day, so the early bird wins. Weekday lunch is the move; closed Sundays and holidays.

📍 Find on Google Maps

Torimeshi Toritoh Bunten 鳥めし 鳥藤分店

Chicken · Oyakodon¥1,000–1,800

In the Tsukiji Outer Market, run by Toritoh, a chicken wholesaler operating since 1906 — whole birds arrive fresh daily and are broken down by craftsmen. Famous for its oyakodon (chicken and silky egg over rice) and chicken rice bowls; there's even a morning set with chicken broth. Open early, lunch only (roughly 7:30–14:00, weekdays).

📍 Find on Google Maps

Sumibiyaki Himono Shokudō Echigoya Yasokichi 炭火焼干物食堂 越後屋 八十吉

Grilled Dried FishLunch ~¥1,000–1,500

A charcoal-grill canteen in an old-folk-house setting near Higashi-Ginza, devoted to himono — Japan's traditional dried fish, selected from around the country at Tsukiji and grilled fast over strong charcoal heat to concentrate the umami. Over 20 set-meal options at lunch; expect a queue of local office workers, always a good sign.

📍 Find on Google Maps

Nihonbashi & Tokyo Station 日本橋・東京駅

Ningyōchō Imahan 人形町今半

Sukiyaki · WagyuLunch ¥3,000–6,000 · Dinner ¥10,000+

Established in 1895 as a beef hot-pot house, and since 1956 a sukiyaki specialist in the charming old Ningyōchō district. Top-grade Kuroge wagyu is simmered tableside in sweet-savory warishita sauce and dipped in raw egg — for many, the definitive sukiyaki experience in Japan, with kimono-clad staff cooking for you.

Friend's note: Probably the most famous sukiyaki restaurant of all.

📍 Find on Google Maps

Hyōki, Yaesu 瓢嘻 八重洲店

Dashi-shabu · Kaiseki · Private RoomsLunch ~¥5,000 · Dinner ~¥10,000

A Kyoto-style restaurant near Tokyo Station where every seat is in a private room — a hushed, ryokan-like atmosphere. The signature is dashi-shabu: top-quality pork swished through fragrant dashi broth rather than plain water, served as part of seasonal kaiseki courses. Lovely for a calm, special-occasion meal.

📍 Find on Google Maps

Ueno 上野 — museums, park & market energy

Kanimura Ueno Honten 海鮮・鮨 食べ放題 かに村 上野本店

Crab · All-you-can-eat Sushi¥7,000–10,000

An all-you-can-eat seafood hall near Okachimachi Station: 100-minute courses of snow crab and red snow crab plus sushi made from 50+ toppings — over 100 dishes on the bigger plans, with seafood BBQ on the top course. Private rooms available; ideal when you want to commit fully to crab.

📍 Find on Google Maps

Ramen Kamo to Negi, Ueno-Okachimachi Honten らーめん 鴨to葱 上野御徒町本店

Duck Ramen¥1,000–1,600

“Duck, green onion, and water — nothing else”: the broth is made daily from whole domestic ducks and carefully chosen negi (Japanese leeks), with zero additives. Clean, deep, and elegant; you can even customize which varieties of negi go in your bowl. The flagship near Ueno-Okachimachi runs around the clock, and the queue moves fast.

Tonkatsu Yamabe, Ueno とんかつ山家 上野店

Tonkatsu · Bargain¥800–1,500

The people's tonkatsu: thick, juicy loin-cutlet set meals from around ¥800–950 — roughly half what famous tonkatsu houses charge, with quality far above the price. Expect a line of locals at lunch near Okachimachi/Ameyoko. Cash only.

📍 Find on Google Maps

Nadai Unatoto, Ueno 名代 宇奈とと 上野店

Unagi (Eel) · Small Chain¥550–2,200

A small chain on a mission to rescue unagi from luxury status — its motto is “cheap, fast, delicious.” Eel is grilled over binchō charcoal with the house soy-based sauce, and a basic unadon (eel over rice) starts at just ¥550, with the popular double at around ¥1,000 — a fraction of what eel usually costs in Japan. The Ueno branch sits near Ameyoko market and runs from morning to late evening.

Friend's note: They actually have far more stores in other places, but the Ueno and Asakusa branches are the most popular among foreigners. The stores are usually not that spacious, but the unagi here is a lot more affordable — and delicious.

Shinjuku 新宿

Shinjuku Omoide Yokochō 新宿西口思い出横丁

Food Alley · Yakitori · Izakaya¥2,000–4,000

Not a single restaurant but a famous narrow lane by Shinjuku Station's west exit — “Memory Lane” — lined on both sides with tiny smoke-filled yakitori stalls and bars, most seating fewer than ten people. Born from the post-war black market, it preserves the atmosphere of 1960s Japan. Squeeze in anywhere that has a seat, order skewers and a beer, and soak it in. Many stalls are cash-only.

Friend's note: A narrow street of restaurants that brings back the memory of 1960s Japan. Official site: shinjuku-omoide.com

📍 Find on Google Maps

Shinjuku Kappo Nakajima 新宿割烹 中嶋

Kappō · Michelin ★Lunch ~¥800–1,000 · Dinner ¥8,000+

Often called the cheapest Michelin-starred meal on Earth. The third-generation chef descends from the head chef of the prestigious 1925 restaurant Hoshigaoka Saryō. At lunch the menu is sardines (iwashi), four ways — sashimi, deep-fried, simmered, or yanagawa-style with egg — for under ¥1,000. No lunch reservations; queue before opening.

📍 Find on Google Maps

Tempura Funabashiya, Shinjuku Honten 天ぷら船橋屋 新宿本店

TempuraLunch ¥1,500–3,000 · Dinner ¥4,000–8,000

A tempura house founded in 1886 on this very spot in Shinjuku. Seasonal seafood and vegetables are fried in additive-free sesame oil pressed by a slow Edo-period technique, giving the batter its signature fragrance. The tendon (tempura over rice) lunch is the classic order at this 130-plus-year-old institution.

📍 Find on Google Maps

Shinjuku Nakamuraya 新宿中村屋

Indian-style Curry · Historic¥1,500–2,500

A piece of history on a plate. In 1927, exiled Indian revolutionary Rash Behari Bose — who had married into the Nakamuraya bakery family that sheltered him — launched “pure Indian curry” here so Japanese diners wouldn't confuse it with British-style curry. The press dubbed it “the taste of love and revolution,” and the same Indo-karii is still served in the flagship building today.

Friend's note: THE Japanese curry.

Shibuya 渋谷

Shibuya Nikuyokochō 渋谷肉横丁

Meat Alley · 25+ Restaurants¥2,000–5,000

Japan's largest “meat alley”: around 25–28 small meat-themed restaurants packed into two floors of the Chitose Kaikan building on Shibuya Center-gai — yakiniku, yakitori, meat sushi, horumon (offal), and more, in a loud, Shōwa-retro bar-hopping atmosphere. Open from noon until 7 a.m. (varies by shop).

Friend's note: Not a single restaurant — more like a specially designed food court. Lots of choice. Official site: nikuyokocho.jp

📍 Find on Google Maps

Hikiniku to Come, Shibuya 挽肉と米 渋谷店

Hamburg Steak · Reservation Only~¥2,000–3,000

Tokyo's viral hamburg-steak sensation, devoted to one dish: charcoal-grilled patties of 100% freshly ground beef served over freshly cooked rice — their motto is “freshly ground, freshly grilled, freshly cooked.” Every seat rings an open kitchen, and three small hamburgs arrive one at a time so each lands on your rice hot off the grill. Walk-ins are not accepted: book online via the official site, where slots for the following week open every Friday at 9:00 a.m. and vanish quickly.

📍 Find on Google Maps

Unagi Yoshikawa, Shibuya うなぎ吉川 渋谷店

Unagi (Eel)¥1,600–3,000

Grilled freshwater eel — usually a luxury dish — at remarkably friendly prices, a few minutes up Dōgenzaka from Shibuya Station. Generous portions (about 1.5× the usual), open 11:00–24:00 daily, with counter seats for solo diners. Try the unatamajū: a whole eel resting on a fluffy four-egg omelette over rice.

Asakusa 浅草

Funachū, Asakusa Hanakawado 鮒忠 浅草花川戸店

Unagi · Yakitori¥2,000–5,000

Founded in 1946 by Tadao Nemoto, remembered as “the father of yakitori,” on a site considered the birthplace of the dish. Two minutes from Asakusa Station, it pairs Edo-period décor with domestic eel: the unajū (eel over rice in a lacquered box) is the classic, and the una-tori-jū combines eel and yakitori in one box — the house's two histories together.

📍 Find on Google Maps

Nadai Unatoto, Asakusa 名代 宇奈とと 浅草店

Unagi (Eel) · Small Chain¥550–2,200

The Asakusa branch of the budget-unagi chain (see the Ueno entry above) — and conveniently on the very same Hanakawado block as Funachū, so the neighborhood is something of an eel pilgrimage stop. Same formula: binchō-charcoal-grilled eel under a sweet-savory glaze, with unadon starting at ¥550 and takeout available. Perfect for an affordable eel fix between Sensō-ji sightseeing stops.

Friend's note: They actually have far more stores in other places, but the Ueno and Asakusa branches are the most popular among foreigners. The stores are usually not that spacious, but the unagi here is a lot more affordable — and delicious.

One Last Thing

最後に
  • Say it like a local: “Itadakimasu” (いただきます) before eating, “Gochisōsama deshita” (ごちそうさまでした) when leaving. Staff will light up.
  • No tipping — ever. It can even cause confusion. Great service is simply the default.
  • Slurping noodles is polite. Genuinely. It cools the noodles and signals enjoyment.
  • If a place looks tiny, full of locals, and slightly intimidating — that's exactly the one to walk into.
